When the heirs of George Sheldon alienated the Dungeon in 1680, the
manor passed to the Lee family, whose later connections lead the inquiry
away from Canterbury to Norfolk and Herefordshire. The published history
identifies Lee-Warner ownership around the beginning of the nineteenth
century. The family's later history supplies candidates for succession,
but it does not yet supply a complete documentary chain for the lordship.
Tracing that chain is the central open problem of this account.

## Henry Lee of the Dane John

The purchaser of 1680 was Henry Lee, esquire (c. 1657-1734), a
Canterbury alderman who sat as Member of Parliament for the city and
whose entry in the History of Parliament describes him as "of Dane
John, nr. Canterbury." Lee resided at the property and rebuilt the
family's fortunes around it. Through his marriage the family came into
the estates of the Warners, the family of John Warner, Bishop of
Rochester, and Lee's son Henry took the additional surname Warner as
a condition of that inheritance, becoming Henry Lee Warner (1688-1760),
of Nackington near Canterbury and later of Walsingham Abbey in Norfolk.

## The Move to Norfolk

The Walsingham inheritance changed the family's relationship to
Canterbury. The Lee Warners became Norfolk gentry, and the Dungeon
became a distant holding administered through tenants. The mansion
house, by then called Donjon alias the Coventry House, was demolished
in 1752 by Thomas Lee Warner, who died possessed of the estate in
1768. His son, Henry Lee Warner of Walsingham Abbey, was the owner
when Edward Hasted completed his survey of Kent around 1800. The
[Hasted archive entry]({{ '/archives/hasted-history-of-kent' | relative_url }})
sets out that published descent. The [surviving-estate page]({{ '/history/surviving-estate' | relative_url }})
distinguishes the mansion's demolition from later clearance of other
buildings.

Two details in the published accounts repay attention. Hasted connects
the patronage of St. Mary Bredin with the manor's owners in his
[history of Canterbury]({{ '/archives/hasted-history-of-canterbury' | relative_url }}).
Ireland later describes the property as Deanjohn Farm in
[England's Topographer]({{ '/archives/irelands-englands-topographer' | relative_url }}).
These are useful routes into the later records. Neither makes every
subsequent church presentation or transaction involving the farm a
proved transfer of the lordship.

## The Tyberton Branch: A Documentary Lead

The Reverend Daniel Henry Lee Warner (1775-1858) married Anne Brydges,
co-heiress of Tyberton Court in Herefordshire. The project's
[research ledger]({{ '/research' | relative_url }}) identifies leases
catalogued as relating to Donjon Farm, Canterbury, dated 1695 and 1814,
among the Tyberton Court muniments at the Herefordshire Archive and
Records Centre, reference **A81/II/237–8**.

That archival connection is worth pursuing. It is not the same as having
read the leases and established what they conveyed. The parties, parcels,
terms, and any express reference to the manor or its lordship still need
to be inspected and transcribed. The date 1814 cannot by itself be
assigned to Daniel Henry's tenure as lord. Nor does the survival of a
document in a family's papers establish that every property mentioned
in it descended through that family.

The Reverend George Brydges Lee Warner (1812-1880), a son of the Tyberton
family, served as vicar of St. Mary Bredin; the parish church's spire was
raised in 1881 as his memorial. This supplies another connection between
the family and Canterbury. The presentation record and the basis on
which patronage was exercised need examination before they can be used
in an argument about the lordship.

## Later Family Members and Presumptive Succession

Robert Henry Lee-Warner (1823-1895) is recorded at Tyberton Court in the
censuses of 1881 and 1891 and in the project's
[Bateman source entry]({{ '/archives/batemans-great-landowners' | relative_url }}).
Major Chandos Brydges Lee-Warner (1863-1944), who married Lady Dorothea
Augusta FitzClarence in 1899, belongs to the later family history.
These are reasons to investigate their papers and estates, not proof
that either held the Dungeon lordship.

The [lords page]({{ '/lords-of-the-manor' | relative_url }}) retains them
as presumptive candidates and identifies the missing evidence. No
conveyance, devise, or other instrument establishing the particular
lordship's passage to either man has been located for this project.
Failure to find a sale out of the family is not positive evidence of
an inheritance within it. A family succession and a property succession
may coincide, but the coincidence has to be demonstrated.

## Land, Patronage, and Lordship

[HM Land Registry's Practice Guide 22](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/manors/practice-guide-22-manors)
distinguishes a lordship title, manorial land, and manorial rights, which
may exist separately. Accordingly, a document concerning the farm needs
to be read for what it actually transfers. Evidence about church patronage
also needs its own documentary basis; Hasted's historical association
cannot simply be extended indefinitely.

This does not discard the Tyberton leads. It makes the task more precise.
A lease can identify a landlord or tenant. A will can identify beneficiaries.
A property schedule can identify the subject of a settlement. To connect
those records into a history of this lordship, the inquiry must establish
both the property concerned and the route by which it passed.

## The Open Question

The question is therefore wider than where the lordship went after 1944.
It is how far its later descent can be documented at all. Chandos Brydges
Lee-Warner's death in that year is a useful point at which to examine
probate records, but his possession of the lordship must not be assumed
as the starting fact. The earlier nineteenth-century links also require
proof.

Possible routes include a family devise, an estate sale, or a separate
assignment. A family recollection of an insurance-company connection
remains unverified and is recorded as a lead, not as an event in the
manor's history. Probate files, property schedules, the Finance Act 1910
valuation records, and estate-sale notices are the next sources to
examine. Evidence of land ownership from any of them will still need
to be distinguished from evidence of the incorporeal lordship.

The [State of the Claim]({{ '/state-of-the-claim' | relative_url }}) records
the present assessment. The [October 2026 research note]({{ '/research/notes/2026-10-12-place-and-evidence' | relative_url }})
explains the clarification made here: the candidates remain worth
investigating, but no newly found deed has closed the chain.
