When Alderman Simmons handed the Dane John back to the city of
Canterbury in 1796, the pleasure garden he had created over six years
and £1,500 of his own money was abruptly without a custodian. The
city managed the transition poorly. A Burghmote committee was eventually
formed in 1803 to take the site in hand, and in 1836 the newly
constituted Canterbury Council assumed permanent oversight. But the
real transformation of the gardens into the Victorian public space
they became, and that they largely remain today, came only when the
city committed to substantial investment in the site's character and
amenities.

## William Masters and the Victorian Redesign

Around 1840, William Masters was engaged to work on the gardens. Masters
was among the most significant figures in Victorian Canterbury's
horticultural world: he owned and operated Masters' Exotic Nursery, a
Canterbury institution that specialized in the unusual and ornamental
plantings that Victorian garden fashion prized. He was also a botanist
of some standing, corresponding with leading figures in British botany
and contributing to the scientific literature on plant varieties.

Masters brought to the Dane John the Victorian sensibility for the
picturesque and the didactic: gardens as spaces that should instruct
and elevate their visitors as well as provide pleasure. His work built
on the framework that Simmons had established in 1790, refining the
plantings and extending the ornamental character of the space. The
formal avenue and winding terrace walk that Simmons had created provided
the underlying structure; Masters added the texture and variety that
Victorian taste demanded.

The design credit for the Dane John Gardens has sometimes been attributed
to Masters alone, obscuring Simmons's foundational role. The more
accurate account is that the gardens represent two phases of
transformation half a century apart, shaped by two men with different
resources and different visions: Simmons's direct, civic generosity
producing a functional public garden, Masters's horticultural expertise
producing the Victorian refinement that made it a destination.

## The Bandstand

Among the most visible Victorian additions to the gardens is the
bandstand, a structure that embodied the era's belief in the
civilizing and improving power of public music. The bandstand culture
of the Victorian period was explicitly connected to ideas about working-class leisure: municipal parks provided spaces for respectable
outdoor recreation that kept people out of the public house, and
bandstands ensured that those spaces had organized, uplifting
entertainment on offer.

The Dane John bandstand, set against the backdrop of the ancient city
walls, became a focal point for the musical and civic life of
Canterbury through the Victorian period and into the twentieth century.
Concerts in the bandstand attracted residents and visitors in numbers
that reinforced the gardens' role as the city's principal public green
space.

## The Fountain and Monuments

The memorial fountain that stands in the gardens dates from the
Victorian period, as do several of the sculptural elements that
contribute to the gardens' character. These additions were part of
a broader pattern of civic embellishment in which Victorian towns
and cities invested in public art and public amenity as expressions
of municipal pride and civic confidence.

The monument to Alderman Simmons, erected in 1803, predates the
Victorian improvements but was incorporated into the Victorian
understanding of the gardens as a place with a history worth
commemorating. The obelisk on the summit of the mound, with its
inscription recording Simmons's generosity in laying out the gardens
at his own expense, served as a visible reminder of the site's origins
within an environment that was progressively becoming something more
elaborate than Simmons had originally created.

## Victorian Canterbury and the Gardens

The Victorian transformation of the Dane John must be understood in
the context of Canterbury's broader development in the nineteenth
century. The arrival of the railway in the 1840s brought new populations
and new commercial energy to the city. The construction of the Chatham
and Dover Railway station in the 1860s, which required the removal of
one of the ancient mounds that Godfrey-Faussett would later identify
as a companion to the Dane John mound, was emblematic of the
Victorian tendency to sacrifice ancient landscape features to the
demands of modern infrastructure.

Against this background, the maintenance and improvement of the Dane
John Gardens represented a choice to preserve and enhance a significant
piece of the city's historic landscape. The Victorian city that built
the railway station and expanded beyond the medieval walls in every
direction also chose to maintain a walled garden around an ancient
mound, to hire a botanist to plant it properly, and to erect a
bandstand so that its citizens could listen to music on summer
afternoons. Both impulses were genuinely Victorian.

## The Gardens Enter the Twentieth Century

By the time of the First World War, the Dane John Gardens had
established the character they retain today: an enclosed historic
garden within the city walls, centered on the ancient mound, with
formal plantings, a bandstand, and a monument that anchored it in
the city's collective memory. The twentieth century added modern
amenities, including the children's play area and updated landscaping,
without fundamentally altering the Victorian-era character of the
space.

The gardens escaped serious bomb damage during the Second World War,
when Canterbury was subjected to the Baedeker Raids of 1942, though
the city around them suffered considerably. The sparing of the Dane
John area preserved one of the most continuous historic landscapes in
a city that lost much of its medieval character to twentieth-century
reconstruction.
