The Norman Conquest of 1066 did not reach Canterbury in a single
dramatic moment. William's army swung inland after the landing at
Pevensey, fought the decisive engagement at Hastings on the fourteenth
of October, and marched from there toward London. Canterbury submitted
before the army arrived, and the city was taken without resistance.
The Normans, pragmatic conquerors and methodical administrators, looked
at what they had acquired and immediately began to assess it.

What they found at Canterbury was a city of real ecclesiastical and
commercial importance sitting on a landscape that had been organized
and reorganized by its inhabitants for the better part of a thousand
years. The mound stood within the southern part of the walled city,
beside its defences. That position helps explain its later military
use; it does not establish the purpose for which the earlier mound
was constructed.

## The Motte-and-Bailey Castle

Historic England's [scheduled-monument account](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1003780)
identifies Dane John as a Norman motte-and-bailey castle incorporating
probable remains of a Roman burial mound. In this form of fortification,
the motte carried a tower, while the adjoining bailey provided an enclosed
space for other buildings. Adapting an earlier mound did not necessarily
mean leaving it unchanged.

The castle's military life appears to have been short. Historic England
places its replacement by the stone castle to the west at about 1130.
The [Kent HER account](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Monument/MKE77155)
suggests an earlier transition around the turn of the eleventh and
twelfth centuries. These summaries support a broad sequence, not an
exact abandonment date. Neither requires an established prehistoric
origin for the motte, or proves what the mound was used for in the
preceding Anglo-Saxon period.

## What the 1981 Excavation Found {#excavated-bailey}

Canterbury Archaeological Trust excavated the former council-office site
at **15a Dane John** in summer **1981**, under site code **DJ81**.
[Kent HER record MKE93130](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Monument/MKE93130/)
describes a substantial ditch interpreted as part of the Norman bailey.
Historic England also reports dimensions of about
**17 metres wide and 3 metres deep**. These are measurements reported
in the published records, not measurements made for this project.

The HER describes silting and later drainage use, followed by garden
soils and landscaping deposits associated with the public gardens.
The excavation therefore connects the castle's history with later uses
of the ground. It does not date every part of the mound itself.

The original report is catalogued as J. Rady's **“Dane John Site” (1982)**,
in the *Canterbury Archaeological Trust Annual Report 1981–82*.
Its [source record, SKE30883](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Source/SKE30883),
locates it in the Trust's library. The HER summary has been reviewed;
the original report and its figures remain to be inspected before
attempting a more detailed reconstruction.

## What the 1996 Survey Adds {#mound-survey}

The [HER's mound record, MKE77155](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Monument/MKE77155),
also summarizes a survey made in **July 1996**. Measured profiles
identified an off-centre upper portion, interpreted as part of Simmons's
later enlargement. Erosion was mapped to assist conservation. Those
observations describe the mound in 1996, not its present condition.

The record cites C. Jarman's **“The Dane John Mound”**, catalogued as
published in **1999** in *Canterbury's Archaeology 1996–1997*.
The [catalogue entry, SKE30886](https://heritage.kent.gov.uk/Source/SKE30886),
is a lead to the original article, not a record of our having inspected
its survey drawings. Survey date, report coverage, and publication date
are different things.

## Lanfranc and the Cathedral

If the Dane John mound saw only a brief Norman interlude, the Cathedral
that dominated the skyline above it was being transformed more
fundamentally. Lanfranc, the great Norman Archbishop appointed by
William, found on his arrival a Saxon cathedral whose architectural
ambitions he considered inadequate. He eradicated the existing
structure to its foundations and built anew on his own Benedictine
plan. In doing so he also reorganized the institutional arrangements
of the Cathedral, separating the Archbishop's residence from the monks'
and creating the two distinct establishments that persisted into the
modern era.

Godfrey-Faussett's 1875 study of Canterbury's development traced the
consequences of Lanfranc's building program for the city's street plan.
To acquire ground for his palace on the western side of the Cathedral,
Lanfranc diverted the old Roman road that had run straight from the
Staple Gate to the river, bending it into the winding course that
became Palace Street and survives today. Canterbury's street plan,
which bears almost no relationship to the Roman plan beneath it, was
shaped decisively by decisions Lanfranc made in the 1070s.

## Domesday and the Feudal Record

The Domesday survey of 1086, commissioned by William to establish a
comprehensive record of landholding across England, provides the earliest
systematic documentary evidence for the property arrangements of Norman
Canterbury. The survey's entry for the city includes references to
burgesses, to lands held in the city ditch (_in fossato civitatis_), and
to the feudal tenure of various Canterbury properties by the great
ecclesiastical institutions and by lay lords.

The Domesday record does not mention the Manor of the Dungeon by name:
the manor as a documented property emerges only in the later medieval
period, associated with the Chiche family in the reigns of Henry II and
his successors. But the Domesday survey established the feudal framework
within which that documentation would eventually occur. The manorial
system that Domesday captured in its 1086 snapshot was the same system
that generated the records of the Chiche family two and three
generations later.

## The City Expands

The immediate post-Conquest period also saw Canterbury physically
enlarged. The western extension of the city walls, incorporating an
area of land that had stood in the old bed of the River Stour, was
added at this time, together with the Westgate that opened onto it.
These additions reflected both the growth of the city's population
and the Normans' characteristic concern for defensible perimeters.

The area associated with the Manor of the Dungeon remained outside the
city walls to the south. The southern portions of the walled city had
always been less densely settled than the northern half, and the ground
beyond the Worthgate continued to be the kind of open land that an
estate like the Dungeon would occupy: close to the city, accessible
to its markets and courts, but separated from it by the wall and by
the relative quiet of the southern approaches.

## The Foundation of the Manorial Record

The Norman period matters for the manor's history in a way that extends
beyond the brief military use of the mound. It was under Norman and
Angevin rule that the English manorial system was systematized into the
form that generated the documentary record through which the manor's
history can be traced. The Chiche family's documented tenure, with its
references to aldermanries held in fee and bailiffs of the city, belongs
to a world of documented landholding that the Normans created. The early
castle was superseded, while the documentary history of the later manor
continued. Archaeological evidence for the castle does not by itself
establish the boundaries or ownership of that manor.
