The case of Hales v. Petit, reported at 1 Plowd. 253 in Edmund Plowden's
_Commentaries_ (1571), arose from the death of Sir James Hales, Justice
of the Court of Common Pleas and lord of the Manor of the Dungeon in
Canterbury.

## Background

Sir James Hales drowned himself in a watercourse near the Dungeon estate in
August 1554. He had refused to seal the device altering the succession in
favor of Lady Jane Grey, and had later insisted at the Kent assizes that
existing religious statutes remained law until Parliament changed them. Under
Mary I he was imprisoned, and after his release he took his own life.

Under the common law of England, a person who willfully killed themselves
was _felo de se_, a felon of themselves, and their property was forfeit
to the Crown. The question before the Court of Exchequer was at what moment
the felony occurred.

## The Legal Question

The court received arguments that an act, to be legally complete, requires
three branches: to act, to do, and to perform. When Sir James entered the
water with intent to drown, all three branches were satisfied at that
moment, while he was still living. His subsequent death was the consequence
of that completed act. The felony was therefore committed during his
lifetime, and the forfeiture of his estate to the Crown was sustained.

## The Shakespeare Connection

Plowden's _Commentaries_ were widely read in Elizabethan legal circles.
The three-branch argument from Hales v. Petit was well known enough that
Shakespeare drew on it in the gravediggers' scene of _Hamlet_
(c. 1600-1601). The First Clown's argument that "an act hath three
branches, it is to act, to do, to perform" directly echoes the language
in Plowden's report, and scholars have long identified this as the source.
